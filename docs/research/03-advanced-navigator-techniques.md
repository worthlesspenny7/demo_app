## 0. The single most important structural fact (answers question 2 up front)

**The Great Race does not ask the navigator to measure distance at all.** The route book supplies the time for every interval. Per the official Event Regulations, the course instructions use the GRIID format (Great Race International Instruction Design) with five columns; column "A" holds CAMEO diagrams of signs/landmarks/intersections, and *"In Column C of the Course Instructions are the interval times which are required to travel between the signs and landmarks at the assigned speeds, and the cumulative times to those points."* (Event Regulations PDF: https://bloximages.chicago2.vip.townnews.com/greatrace.com/content/tncms/assets/v3/editorial/c/dd/cdd7fcee-d4f6-43ba-8535-064d9ce6112e/6931b4f9a700a.pdf.pdf ; also summarized at https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-crosses-maryland-part-2/).

Ron Rowland (multi-year Great Race navigator, see section 1) states the same: *"Column C of the Great Race instructions contains the speed changes and timing information teams must execute precisely to stay on time."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/ ; https://ronrowland.com/2025-great-race-team-2-graf-rowland/).

So the navigator's core job is **time-keeping against a pre-computed "ghost car" schedule, while the driver holds the assigned speed exactly.** The event's own description: *"The goal is to exactly mimic to the second the 'ghost car' represented by the instructions. Actual cars are always trying to catch up to the ghost car, since the ghost accelerates and decelerates instantaneously, while actual old cars always lose time in each maneuver. So each team must calculate this loss and try to compensate."* (https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/). *"The 'perfect car' accelerates instantaneously from zero to 22 mph, but a real old car may take many seconds to get up to the set speed."* (snippet returned for https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-crosses-maryland-part-2/ and https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/team-hagerty-great-race-2018/ ; exact page of this sentence UNVERIFIED between the two).

Because the odometer is covered/removed and no calculator is allowed (section 7), **the only arithmetic the navigator does in the car is addition/subtraction of seconds** (stop losses, running early/late, calibration correction) on top of the printed times. This reframes what to practice: not speed×time=distance, but (a) flawless stopwatch handling against printed intervals, (b) knowing the car's time losses by heart, (c) keeping an accurate running early/late number, and (d) catching instruction-reading errors.

### 1.7 Consensus "what wins" list (synthesized from the above)
1. Perfect speedometer calibration every morning, and applying the day's correction to every instruction (sections 2.2, 4.2).
2. Knowing the car's acceleration/deceleration time losses cold, from hours/days of pre-event measurement (section 2.3).
3. Never missing an instruction — "attention to detail and the ability to follow instructions accurately" is the officially stated #1 skill.
4. A stopwatch that is always in hand; instant start/stop; lap/split and time-of-day functions.
5. Consistent routines and callouts between driver and navigator; trust (section 6).
6. Consistency across all legs for nine days (the Sharps' edge) rather than occasional perfection — a few seconds per leg over ~40+ legs is what separates places.

## 2. Running a leg when there is no odometer (Great Race model)

### 2.1 The daily flow
- Instructions are handed out shortly before the start: Ron Rowland says *"instructions provided only 30 minutes before the start time"* (https://ronrowland.com/2025-great-race-team-2-graf-rowland/); the Montgomery Community Media writeup says *"20 minutes before each day's start"* (https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/). Treat as 20–30 min; the exact figure for a given year is UNVERIFIED. Cars leave one minute apart (https://ronrowland.com/2025-great-race-team-2-graf-rowland/).
- Volume: *"usually 220 to 250 such instructions per day"* covering *"every turn, speed change, stop, and start"* (https://www.greatrace.com/faq/ ; also https://cokertire.com/great-race-101). Typically four to seven legs per day (https://www.greatrace.com/news/ace-award.html).
- Example instruction (press description): *"turn right at the stop sign, go exactly 25 mph for 40 seconds, then increase to 45 mph and turn right at the first paved road."* (https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/). Ron Rowland's example of a timed speed change: *"go 30MPH for 36 seconds, then go 40MPH."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/).
- Each morning starts with a speedometer calibration run, then the team goes "on the clock": *"The first miles each day are pretty much a tire-warmup and speedometer-calibration run, during which teams hold the speed at exactly 50 mph for an extended stretch of highway... The route book gives exact times teams should arrive at given landmarks or signs, and based on actual arrival times versus the ideal, teams can correct the speedometer... then they go 'on the clock'."* (https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/team-hagerty-great-race-2018/). A press/participant account describes the calibration as a *"15-30 minute interstate highway run at 50 mph"* and warns *"if you're off 1 mile an hour on the speedometer that can put you 10 to 15 seconds off for the day"* (snippet returned for the group https://sbj.net/stories/mace-and-the-great-race,9134 , https://lcnme.com/266503/currentnews/local-racer-focuses-on-endurance-and-precision-in-nine-day-road-rally/ , https://fifthaveinternetgarage.blogspot.com/2015/10/the-great-racehow-it-works.html ; exact source UNVERIFIED). Rowland: *"The speedometer calibration run is typically 20 minutes or longer with a dozen or more interval readings along the way."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/).

### 2.2 Applying the calibration result all day
- Rowland: *"After completion of the speedometer calibration run, if your clock time shows an actual time compared to the correct time, this is an error that must be calculated and applied to every instruction completed throughout the day."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/). In other words the morning run yields a seconds-per-minute (or percentage) correction that the navigator then either (a) folds into every printed time or (b) converts into a speedometer offset for the driver.
- The historical (pre-Timewise) version of (b): *"On their first day of competition running with the speedometer needle on 50, their times were early. The next day, running at 49 MPH for an assigned speed of 50 resulted in late times. They then tried 49.5 MPH and at the end of the speedometer check were only off by a few seconds, and could adjust for that every 10-15 minutes when on the clock."* (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/life-before-the-timewise-speedometer/article_141f8207-df06-4a3a-b344-b375aaeb1df6.html). Lesson: residual drift is handled as a small periodic correction rather than a continuous one.
- Modern practice: the Timewise 825 is *"the official speedometer of the Great Race"*; it has a 4-inch dial using all 360° for the speed scale, *"approximately 0.25 inches (6 mm) between each single mile per hour indicator mark"*, and is calibrated by a four-digit factor = inches travelled per 10 transducer pulses to 0.1 inch (e.g. 531.4 in → 5314) (https://stevemckelvie.wordpress.com/2014/06/14/timewise-825-speedometer-a-great-tool-for-the-great-race/ ; http://www.timewise.us/products/825.html). Steve McKelvie: *"calibratable speedometers are required for good scores in time-speed rallies."* (same McKelvie post).

### 2.3 Stops, pauses and the car's "performance data" (personal car calibration)
- Rowland: *"The most common use of a stopwatch in the Great Race is measuring the pause time at stop signs and traffic lights. Although the instructions typically provide a 15-second pause, the time lost when the vehicle stops and accelerates back to speed must be accounted for."* and *"a countdown to begin accelerating [is] critical."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/).
- The worked number he gives: *"From your practice time you will know that accelerating from 0 to 40 mph results in a net time lost of 4.5 seconds."* (same page). So the navigator's stop procedure is: on wheels-stopped, start the pause countdown; subtract the known acceleration loss (e.g. 4.5 s for 0→40) from the 15 s allowance; tell the driver to go at ~10.5 s so that the car is back at 40 mph at the moment the ghost car would be. (The subtraction mechanics are my inference from his numbers; the numbers themselves are sourced.)
- Deceleration cost is handled the same way: the ghost car decelerates instantly, the real car does not, so a stop also *gains* time on the approach (the car is still moving while the ghost is stopped) — Team 39's method below treats entry speed and exit speed as separate parameters for exactly this reason.
- Where to get the numbers: *"Great Racers spending hours, sometimes days, collecting performance data"* (https://ronrowland.com/2025-great-race-team-2-graf-rowland/); Rookie School sessions *"involve building performance data that teams use during the race to determine time lost during each maneuver"* (snippet; UNVERIFIED exact source, see 1.6). The regulations explicitly permit *"vehicle performance notes, speedometer performance notes, race tables"* in the car (Event Regulations PDF, URL in section 0).
- Where exactly a speed change is executed (sign vs. front bumper): **UNVERIFIED** — no snippet addressed the reference point. SCCA general instructions normally define execution at the point where the car's front bumper is abreast of the sign/landmark (general TSD knowledge; UNVERIFIED for Great Race specifically; the GRIID definitions in the regulations PDF would be the authoritative source).

### 2.4 Team 39's alternative: "continuous error offset" with Mark! callouts
John Corey's "Dialing It In" post describes *"a method of continuous error offset instead of using traditional acceleration and stop times, using a 15-second stop example with entry/exit speeds of 20 and 40 mph. The navigator starts a stopwatch when the driver calls out 'Mark!' and stops the watch at the driver's second mark."* (https://www.greatrace.com/news/dialing-it-in-team-39-corey-corey.html). Interpretation (mine): rather than pre-computing per-maneuver losses, the driver marks the start and end of each maneuver, the navigator measures the actual elapsed time, compares it to what the ghost car would have taken, and carries the difference forward as a running offset to be burned off. Note the honest follow-ups: on one day they were *"over 2 minutes total error across five legs, with each leg being early by 10-30 seconds"* even though *"they were making frequent stops and corrections"* (https://greatrace.com/john-corey/so-bad-it-isnt-even-wrong.html). Consistent early errors of 10–30 s per leg point to a systematic bias (calibration or per-stop assumptions), which is why experts stress fixing the model (2.2/2.3) before adding corrections.

### 2.5 Speed changes with a stopwatch
- Rowland: *"A stopwatch is vital when executing timed speed change instructions in the Great Race. A typical instruction of this type might be: go 30MPH for 36 seconds, then go 40MPH."* (https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/). The navigator starts the watch at the point the 30 mph begins and calls the change at 36 s, adjusted for the car's known acceleration loss between 30 and 40 (so the call is made a little early).
- "The Tale of Two Navigators": the navigator *"count[s] down speed changes and stops while the driver watches the speedometer"* — a countdown ("5-4-3-2-1-go") rather than a single word, so the driver can begin the throttle input exactly on zero (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/the-tale-of-two-navigators/article_25f53418-3594-4d83-88a1-32579b275878.html).

## 3. Clock technique

### 3.1 Permitted timing kit shapes the technique
Per the Event Regulations (URL in section 0): *"Each Team may have one approved digital or analog stopwatch"*; *"One mechanical or quartz-crystal analog time-of-day watch or clock is the primary timepiece... The clock must not have stopwatch, compensating, calculating, or split-action functions, or digital readout"*; each crew member may wear *"one approved analog wristwatch without stopwatch function"*; *"a digital wristwatch is prohibited"*; any clock *"self-correcting or self-synchronizing by using radio, cellular, or satellite/GPS technology is prohibited."* So a Great Race navigator has exactly: **one stopwatch + one analog time-of-day clock + (optionally) an analog wristwatch**, plus the Timewise speedometer. The "two or three stopwatches" layouts common in other rallies are therefore **not** available in the Great Race; the single stopwatch's lap/split function substitutes.

### 3.2 The five stopwatch jobs (Rowland)
From https://ronrowland.com/analog-stopwatch-with-countdown-bezel-application-notes/ : *"Common stopwatch applications in the Great Race include pause time countdown, timed speed changes, running early/late adjustments, transit time countdown, and speedometer calibration."* Also: *"a stopwatch capable of providing a split-time reading is desirable, although not required"* for the calibration run (so you can read interval times at each landmark without stopping the overall timer).

### 3.3 Time-of-day clock with rotating bezel
- Rowland uses a rally clock with a rotating bezel to handle out-times: he *"uses a stopwatch to keep track of transit time while aligning the clock bezel with the adjusted out time seconds"* (https://ronrowland.com/rally-clock-with-rotating-bezel-application-notes/). I.e. the bezel is turned so its zero sits at the seconds value of the next required time, giving an at-a-glance countdown on an analog dial without arithmetic.
- His 3D-printed rally clock design: 7-inch overall (6-inch face + bezel), analog hands, Seiko quartz movement, dual-function rotating bezel, on/off switch, 16 oz, battery, *"no automatic time synchronization capability... time adjustments are performed manually"* to stay Great Race legal (https://ronrowland.com/this-rally-clock-has-everything/).
- Grand Champion navigators *"prefer to use the accuracy of the stopwatch for 'time of day' starts during the rally"* (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/the-tale-of-two-navigators/article_25f53418-3594-4d83-88a1-32579b275878.html) — i.e. start the stopwatch on the official out-time and run leg time from it, rather than reading an analog sweep hand.
- Heuer dash-clock history note: *"Rally clocks were for the navigator, not the driver, and were mounted on the other side of the cockpit, away from the driver's instruments."* (https://www.onthedash.com/heuers-innovation-the-rotating-tachymeter-bezel-for-race-timing/).

### 3.4 Stopwatch ergonomics
- Always in hand on a lanyard, for instant start/stop (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/the-tale-of-two-navigators/article_25f53418-3594-4d83-88a1-32579b275878.html ; https://www.greatrace.com/do-not-import/rally-equipment/article_ff91468f-1970-5309-9d7f-6e2ad9d319a2.html). Lap/split + time-of-day functions recommended (same Rally Equipment page).
- Analog watch choices among Great Racers: Steve McKelvie (Great Race competitor and Timewise reseller) describes a Hanhart whose second hand makes *"one complete rotation... 30 seconds rather than 60 seconds... making each second on the face wider and easier to read to the 0.1 second"*, and a rattrapante (split-seconds) model that *"has two second hands and can therefore time multiple simultaneous events, allowing one to stop the 'rattrapante' hand to note the time of a specific event without completely stopping the stopwatch... for example during a continuous speedometer calibration run during the Great Race."* (https://stevemckelvie.wordpress.com/2016/12/10/my-new-hanhart-stopwatch-has-special-capabilities/ ; see also https://stevemckelvie.wordpress.com/2015/05/11/my-heuer-monte-carlo-stopwatch/).
- Multi-stopwatch boards (relevant to non-Great-Race TSD where allowed): a timing-board method with three watches so that *"you always have one stopwatch stopped on your elapsed time for the previous mile, one stopwatch showing your running time for the current mile, and one stopwatch resting at zero, ready to start timing the next mile."* This source is a rowing head-race timing page (https://www.headracetiming.com/resources/watches.aspx), not a rally page; its applicability to rally is by analogy only.

### 3.5 "Driving to the clock" vs "driving to the speedometer"
- Great Race practice as described is a hybrid: the driver drives to the speedometer (holds the exact CAST), the navigator drives to the clock (compares elapsed time at each landmark to Column C cumulative time) and issues corrections. "The Tale of Two Navigators" puts it as the navigator counting down *"while the driver watches the speedometer and tries to hold the correct speeds"* (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/the-tale-of-two-navigators/article_25f53418-3594-4d83-88a1-32579b275878.html). Hagerty: the goal is *"holding speed accurately and accelerating consistently"* (https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/team-hagerty-great-race-2018/).
- Targa-timing analogy (Wikipedia): *"if a competitor is exactly on time, the clock will show exactly the same time at every time control they visit, making it very easy to see whether they are ahead or behind, and if so by how much"* (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targa_timing) — the rotating-bezel trick in 3.3 achieves the same at-a-glance ahead/behind reading on an ordinary clock.

## 4. Mental math under time pressure

### 4.1 What arithmetic is actually needed in the Great Race
Because Column C prints interval and cumulative times (section 0) and calculators are banned (section 7), the in-car math reduces to:
1. Adding the day's calibration correction to printed times (2.2).
2. Subtracting the car's known maneuver losses from pause allowances and speed-change times (2.3, 2.5).
3. Keeping a running early/late figure in seconds and converting it to a speed/time correction (4.3).
4. Time-of-day arithmetic for out-times (3.3).

### 4.2 Pre-computed sheets
- The regulations permit *"Pencils, pens, scratch pads, vehicle performance notes, speedometer performance notes, race tables, instructions, and maps furnished by Great Race"* (Event Regulations PDF, URL in section 0). "Race tables" and "performance notes" are therefore the legal substitutes for a calculator: tables of time-lost per maneuver by entry/exit speed, and correction-per-minute tables for calibration offsets.
- Rowland's "Rally Rule #14: Sanity Check Your Charts" (https://ronrowland.com/rally-rule-14-sanity-check-your-charts/) — title indicates charts are central and must be verified before use; content UNVERIFIED.

### 4.3 Making up time (the 10%/20% rule)
- Snippet (returned for the Great Race blog group https://www.greatrace.com/archive/navigator-notes/article_c7d5d429-3c3a-5fb1-aaf2-85518a2c7084.html , https://www.greatrace.com/blog/life-before-the-timewise-speedometer/article_141f8207-df06-4a3a-b344-b375aaeb1df6.html , https://www.traynorstravels.com/blog/the-great-race ; exact source UNVERIFIED): *"When delayed at a stop sign, drivers can make up time by going 10% over the normal speed for 10 times the delay, or 20% overspeed for 5 times the delay."* Check: at speed v for the ghost, running 1.1v for duration T covers the ghost's distance in T/1.1, saving ≈0.091T; for a delay d you need T ≈ 11d — the "10×" rule is a slight under-correction, but is the standard field approximation. At 1.2v, time saved = T(1 − 1/1.2) = T/6, so T = 6d; the "5×" rule again slightly under-corrects. These are deliberately conservative so the team does not overshoot into being early.
- The pre-Timewise account shows the other direction: trimming to 49.5 mph for an assigned 50 and *"adjust[ing] for that every 10-15 minutes when on the clock"* (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/life-before-the-timewise-speedometer/article_141f8207-df06-4a3a-b344-b375aaeb1df6.html).

### 4.4 Non-Great-Race mental tools (general TSD knowledge; UNVERIFIED in this session — no snippets retrieved)
- Seconds-per-mile table: 3600/CAST (e.g. 30 mph = 120 s/mi, 36 mph = 100 s/mi, 40 mph = 90 s/mi, 45 mph = 80 s/mi, 50 mph = 72 s/mi, 60 mph = 60 s/mi). "Friendly" CASTs are those dividing 3600 evenly.
- Speed-factor cards (minutes per mile = 60/CAST) for odometer-equipped rallies.
- SCCA definitions (CAST = "Change Average Speed To") are in Scott Harvey's "Road Rally 104 – Sample General Instructions" (http://drscca.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/11/road_rally_104.pdf) and San Diego Region SCCA Generals (https://sdrscca.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/San-Diego-Region-SCCA-Road-Rally-GENERAL-INSTRUCTIONS-1.pdf) — these two URLs surfaced in results but their content was not retrieved.

## 5. Error recovery under pressure

### 5.1 Why zeroing matters and penalties are symmetric
- *"Each second early or late at a Timing Checkpoint compared to the computed perfect time results in 1 second penalty."* and a car arriving *"more than 30 minutes after the computed cumulative perfect time from the most recent Stage Start or Restart will be scored as having missed the Checkpoint."* (Event Regulations PDF, URL in section 0; also https://www.greatrace.com/faq/). Early = late, so the optimal strategy is to aim at zero, not "a little late to be safe".
- An "Ace" is a perfect (0 s) leg; usually four to seven legs per day (https://www.greatrace.com/news/ace-award.html ; https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/). The Ace sticker existed by 2005 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Race_(classic_rally)).
- Winning totals are *"only a minute or two off the perfect time"* across the event (https://www.greatrace.com/faq/), so a single 30 s blunder is the difference between first and fifth.

### 5.2 Documented failure modes and responses
- Systematic early bias: Team 39's day of *"over 2 minutes total error... each of the five legs being early by 10-30 seconds"* despite *"frequent stops and corrections"* (https://greatrace.com/john-corey/so-bad-it-isnt-even-wrong.html). Response implied by the title and later posts ("2 out of 3 Ain't Bad", "Well, We're Done"): stop micro-correcting, re-derive the model.
- Traffic: Doug Sharp names *"dealing with typical traffic patterns"* as the hardest part of navigating (section 1.1). Team 39 recounts a pickup that *"made a sudden right turn without signaling in front of them, forcing them to swerve and brake hard"* (https://greatrace.com/john-corey/so-bad-it-isnt-even-wrong.html) — unplanned braking costs time that must be re-measured and burned off with the 10%/20% rule (4.3).
- Being behind a slow vehicle, a traffic light, a missed turn, a misread instruction, a lost or mis-pressed stopwatch: no expert quotes were retrievable in this session; the following is standard TSD practice and **UNVERIFIED** here: (a) a navigator's first job after any disruption is to re-establish *when the ghost car was at a known landmark* (from Column C cumulative time + start time) and restart the running early/late count from that landmark; (b) for a missed turn, return to the point of error, measure the time lost with the stopwatch, and treat it as a delay; (c) for a traffic light, time the stop with the watch and apply the car's acceleration loss just as for a stop sign; (d) a lost stopwatch count is recovered from the time-of-day clock plus the Column C cumulative time — which is why experts insist on "time-of-day starts" (3.3) so the clock, not the stopwatch, is the source of truth.
- Decision rule on making up time vs accepting the loss: the 10%/20% rule (4.3) only applies where the overspeed is safe and legal; the Great Race's first "S" is Safety First (https://www.greatrace.com/driver-navigator-basics). UNVERIFIED beyond that.

## 6. Team dynamics

- Ron Rowland: *"Good communication, consistent routines, and trust between the driver and navigator matter just as much as the car."* (https://ronrowland.com/2025-great-race-team-2-graf-rowland/).
- Great Race coaching: Four S's and explicit "communication strategies" are the first items in Driver and Navigator Basics (https://www.greatrace.com/driver-navigator-basics).
- Division of labor: *"the stopwatch is a critical tool for navigators because they use it for counting down speed changes and stops while the driver watches the speedometer and tries to hold the correct speeds"* (https://www.greatrace.com/blog/the-tale-of-two-navigators/article_25f53418-3594-4d83-88a1-32579b275878.html). Driver's job is speed and the car; navigator's job is time and the book.
- Callouts: Team 39's protocol has the **driver** call *"Mark!"* at the start and end of a maneuver while the navigator works the watch (https://www.greatrace.com/news/dialing-it-in-team-39-corey-corey.html) — the person whose hands/feet make the event happen is the one who timestamps it.
- Family teams: the Sharps (father/son, since 1998 — https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/father-and-son-team-win-the-great-race-after-9-days-of-rallying/), Fredettes (father/son, a grandfather's wish — https://www.hagerty.com/media/events/fredette-family/), Gentrys (sisters coached by their mother, who also raced — https://www.times-herald.com/news/local/great-race-a-family-tradition/article_287ddb64-9c06-57b7-8f9b-f63cc6b4d3e4.html), Coreys (father/son, https://www.greatrace.com/news/john-and-ethan-coreys-volvo-adventures-in-rolling-magazine.html). The common thread in the snippets is long continuity (same pair for 10–25 years) rather than any stated communication trick; Olivia Gentry's "the key... is a great navigator" and the sisters' habit of taking every question to their mother are the only explicit dynamics quotes retrieved.
- "Say-back"/confirmation protocols: **UNVERIFIED** — no Great Race source retrieved. Standard practice in TSD and aviation is read-back of the instruction ("Right at stop, CAST 25" / "Right at stop, 25").

## 7. Tools and the rules debate

### 7.1 What the Great Race actually allows (Event Regulations PDF, URL in section 0)
- **Allowed:** one digital *or* analog stopwatch per team; one analog time-of-day clock/watch (no stopwatch, compensating, calculating or split-action functions, no digital readout, no radio/cell/GPS sync); one analog non-stopwatch wristwatch per crew member; calibratable speedometer (Timewise 825 is the official unit — https://stevemckelvie.wordpress.com/2014/06/14/timewise-825-speedometer-a-great-tool-for-the-great-race/); *"Pencils, pens, scratch pads, vehicle performance notes, speedometer performance notes, race tables, instructions, and maps furnished by Great Race."*
- **Prohibited:** *"a calculator, computer, iPad, short-wave/WWV radio, GPS device, etc."*; cellular phone clock/calculator/mapping functions *"at all times"*; laptops/tablets; any radio/cellular/satellite positioning or communication device other than the emergency phone; digital wristwatches. Officials *"may require the modification or removal of any odometer"*; press accounts say odometers are taped over (https://grassrootsmotorsports.com/forum/off-topic-discussion/tsd-rally-no-odometer/72873/page1/ ; https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/).
- Older press summaries saying only an "analog stopwatch" is allowed (https://www.mymcmedia.org/the-great-race-the-coolest-car-event-you-never-heard-of-part-1/) are superseded by the current regulation text ("digital or analog stopwatch"). Whether digital stopwatches were previously disallowed is UNVERIFIED.

### 7.2 What top navigators actually use (from snippets)
- Stopwatch on a lanyard, constantly in hand, with lap/split and time-of-day functions (section 3.4).
- Analog rally clock with rotating bezel on the navigator's side of the dash (Rowland; Heuer tradition) (section 3.3).
- Timewise 825 speedometer, calibrated each morning (section 2.2).
- Hand-built performance tables / race tables / scratch pad (sections 2.3, 4.2).
- Lapboard/clipboard for the instruction sheets (https://www.greatrace.com/do-not-import/rally-equipment/article_ff91468f-1970-5309-9d7f-6e2ad9d319a2.html).
- Not usable in the Great Race: Richta GPS checkpoints/apps, Timewise or Alfano rally computers/odometers, Brantz/Terratrip tripmeters, GPS odometer apps (https://apps.apple.com/us/app/gps-rally-odometer/id1072411708 appears in results as a TSD tool, but GPS devices are prohibited in the Great Race). These are standard in SCCA RoadRally equipped classes and most European regularity events — UNVERIFIED in this session beyond general knowledge.
- Route-book marking (highlighters, pre-reading during the 20–30 min window): UNVERIFIED — no snippet described a marking system, though the time window itself is sourced (2.1).

## 9. Implications for a training simulator (prioritized)

Priority is ordered by (a) how often the skill is exercised per Great Race day (~220–250 instructions, 4–7 legs) and (b) how directly the sourced experts named it.

1. **Stopwatch start/stop/lap on a landmark cue (reaction + consistency).** Every instruction depends on it; "always in hand, instant start/stop" is the stated Grand Champion habit (3.4). *Drill:* a landmark (sign/CAMEO) scrolls into view at the assigned speed; the trainee hits START/LAP when the front bumper is abreast. Score the absolute error and its *consistency* (standard deviation), because a constant bias calibrates out but jitter does not. Add a "both hands busy" variant (holding the lapboard) and a lanyard-in-hand variant.

2. **Pause/stop execution with the car's loss model.** The single most common stopwatch use (2.3). *Drill:* instruction "STOP, pause 15 s" with a known car profile (e.g. 0→40 loses 4.5 s). Trainee must start the countdown on wheels-stop and call "GO" at the right moment; score the time at which the car regains CAST versus the ghost car. Vary entry/exit speeds (Team 39's 20/40 example) and randomize the car profile between sessions so the trainee learns to *read the table*, not memorize one number.

3. **Timed speed changes ("go 30 for 36 s, then 40").** *Drill:* a chain of 5–10 timed CAST changes; trainee counts down to the driver (voice or key) with the acceleration loss pre-subtracted; score each change point. Include changes where the printed interval is short (<20 s) to force the "start the watch before you finish reading" habit.

4. **Morning calibration run and all-day correction.** *Drill:* a 20-minute 50-mph run with a dozen landmarks and Column C times; the simulated speedometer is secretly off by ±0.3–1.5 mph. Trainee must (a) read split times at landmarks, (b) compute the seconds-per-minute error, (c) choose either a speedometer offset (the 49.5-mph method) or a time correction, (d) apply it to the next leg. Score the resulting leg error. This is where "1 mph = 10–15 s/day" bites (2.1).

5. **Running early/late bookkeeping and the 10%/20% recovery rule.** *Drill:* inject disruptions (slow truck for 20 s, a 35-s traffic light, a sudden brake) and show the time lost; trainee must state the new running offset and choose an overspeed plan (10% for 10×, or 20% for 5×), then confirm the offset decays to zero by the next landmark. Score for both residual error and for *not overshooting into early* (penalties are symmetric, 5.1).

6. **Instruction reading accuracy under load ("attention to detail").** The official #1 skill (1.6). *Drill:* a GRIID-style five-column sheet with CAMEO diagrams; the trainee has 20–30 minutes (sourced window, 2.1) to pre-read, highlight stops and speed changes, and write pre-computed "GO" times next to each pause; then run the leg with occasional decoy signs. Score missed/incorrect executions separately from timing error.

7. **Time-of-day out-time arithmetic with an analog clock + bezel.** *Drill:* given an out-time with seconds, set a virtual rotating bezel, start the stopwatch on the out-time, and verify the first landmark time; include minute-rollover and hour-rollover cases. Score setup errors (these are typically whole-minute catastrophes, not seconds).

8. **Recovery after a stopwatch loss or mis-press.** *Drill:* mid-leg, the stopwatch is "reset" by the simulator; trainee must re-derive elapsed time from the time-of-day clock and Column C cumulative time at the next landmark and restart the count. Score time-to-recovery and residual error.

9. **Driver/navigator protocol.** Two-seat mode (or a scripted virtual driver): navigator must deliver "callout → driver read-back → countdown → MARK" with the driver's "Mark!" timestamping maneuvers (Team 39 protocol, 2.4/6). Score dropped read-backs and late calls. Include a "driver overshoots the speed" scenario where the navigator must detect it from the clock, not the speedometer.

10. **Consistency across a full nine-leg day.** A fatigue/endurance mode: run 6 legs back-to-back with 220+ instructions; score the *sum* of absolute errors and the number of Aces, matching how the Great Race is actually won (1.1, 5.1).

Lower priority for the Great Race (but needed for SCCA/regularity modes): seconds-per-mile and speed-factor tables, multi-stopwatch board handling, odometer/tripmeter correction, Targa-timing clock reading.
